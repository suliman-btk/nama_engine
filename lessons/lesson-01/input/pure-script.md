Let's begin by understanding why project management matters.

## Scene 002 — What prime Is

prime is NAMA Foundation's practical course for understanding how development projects move from an idea to real results. It is based on Project DPro, a project management approach for development and humanitarian work. Project DPro does not focus only on schedules or documents. It brings together tools and processes that help teams define an appropriate intervention, organize the work, manage resources and stakeholders, respond to risk and change, and deliver meaningful results. Here, we will explain those ideas through practical project situations. NAMA Foundation is the organization presenting this course to people involved in development and community initiatives.

## Scene 003 — Development Happens Through Projects

Think about the kind of work development organizations do. They may rehabilitate a school, provide clean water, establish health services, distribute food, or support livelihoods. Different sectors and problems, but much of this work happens through projects. Teams develop proposals, make plans, carry out activities, monitor progress, and evaluate results. Project management is part of what helps turn resources and good intentions into organized action and useful results for communities. The way a project is managed affects whether that work reaches the people it is meant to serve.

## Scene 004 — So, What Is a Project?

Before going further, we need one simple idea. A project is temporary and exists to create a specific product, service, or result. It has a beginning, an end, resources to manage, and something it is expected to achieve. Project management means planning, organizing, and managing those resources to reach the intended outputs and outcomes. A project manager does not personally do every activity. They help team members, partners, contractors, community groups, donors, and other stakeholders work together toward the result. That coordination gives the team a way to see the overall result, not just individual tasks.

## Scene 005 — Why Development Projects Are Different

Managing a development project can become complicated quickly. Resources may be limited. Funding can change. Suppliers may fail. Communities, government agencies, partners, contractors, and donors may have different expectations. And development projects often aim for something harder to measure than a physical product: a change in behaviour, well-being, opportunity, or social conditions. This is why good project management matters. We need to understand the context, prepare for uncertainty, and notice when the original plan needs a careful second look.

## Scene 006 — A Case From Project DPro

For practical examples throughout prime, we will draw from the Project DPro guides. One recurring case is the Delta River. An assessment found that poor river water quality was linked to lower crop production and income among farming families, as well as more water-borne illness, especially among poorer families and young children. The case points to several factors: waste dumped into the river, limited access to sanitation facilities, low public awareness, and inadequate wastewater treatment. Before choosing an intervention, we need to understand which parts of that situation a project could realistically address.

## Scene 007 — Do Not Start With the Solution

The Delta River case shows why a project begins with questions. If you see polluted water and immediately say, "Let's build latrines," you may have picked one useful response, but you have not yet understood the whole problem. Waste disposal, sanitation, public awareness, oversight, and treatment could all matter. Who is affected? What evidence do we have? Which causes can this project influence? Tools in prime will help you turn those questions into clearer choices, so the chosen activities follow from the need rather than from an early assumption.

## Scene 008 — Project Management Is About Balance

Projects also have limits. You cannot normally increase activities, shorten deadlines, reduce spending, and expect everything else to stay the same. Project DPro connects scope and quality, cost and resources, and time and schedule. A change to one can affect the others. In a distribution example from the guide, increasing the number of families receiving supplies from ten thousand to twelve thousand changes the scope. The team must then revisit the time and resources needed. Good project management makes those trade-offs visible before a decision is made.

## Scene 009 — Tools Are Only Half of the Job

You will learn tools in prime, but good management is not only about templates, budgets, or schedules. Project DPro describes it as both an art and a science. The science includes estimating, planning, measuring, and tracking work. The art includes listening, communicating, leading, motivating, and resolving conflict. A project can have a strong plan and still struggle if people cannot work well together. Effective project management needs both sides. For example, a useful schedule only works when the team can raise a problem early and discuss what to change.

## Scene 010 — An Education Example From the Guide

The guide offers an example. David manages an education project that provides remedial tutoring to students who stopped attending school, helping them return to the school system. His technical work is strong: reports, deadlines, and planning matter to him. Yet the project faces staff turnover. His team feels that he mainly speaks to them when he wants a task finished or needs to point out a mistake. The lesson is simple: managing the schedule matters, and so does managing the people doing the work. Understanding why colleagues are leaving is therefore as important as seeing the delay on a timeline.

## Scene 011 — Your 14-Lesson Journey

prime contains 14 connected lessons. Lessons 2 to 4 help you understand the approach, gather evidence, and examine the people and problems behind a project idea. Lessons 5 to 7 help you turn that understanding into a project design and set up clear responsibilities and decisions. In Lessons 8 to 11, you develop the activities, schedule, and supporting plans. Lessons 12 to 14 focus on managing the work, responding to change, and closing and handing over the project responsibly. Each part builds on the questions raised by the one before it.

## Scene 012 — What You Should Take From prime

By the end of prime, you should be able to look at a development project differently. Is it addressing the right need? Are the right people involved? Do its activities connect logically to its results? Is the plan realistic? Are risks and evidence guiding decisions? You will see a Problem Tree, Logframe, Work Breakdown Structure, risk register, and Gantt chart, and understand what each helps a team decide. The tools matter because they help people make informed choices together. In Lesson 2, we will look at the management approach connecting all these choices. That's it for today. I'll see you there.
