You've spent the last few lessons learning who you are, what strengths you bring, and why different people make a team stronger. Now, we can name the skill that turns those ideas into action: leadership. The source curriculum gives us a simple working definition. Leadership is directing people toward a goal. Notice what that definition does not say. It does not say having a title, being the loudest person, or giving orders. Leadership is about helping people move toward something they are trying to achieve. For a community leader, the first question is not, am I important? It is, can I help people move toward a useful goal?

The curriculum also makes an important point. Leadership is not reserved for one special personality. Its message is that leadership can be acquired and developed. Some people may feel naturally comfortable taking the lead, while others build that ability through experience, practice, and responsibility. That means you should not decide, I'm just not a leader, because you are quiet, careful, creative, or people focused. Those traits can all appear in leadership. The better question is, how do I naturally influence a group, and what leadership skills do I still need to strengthen? Community leadership grows through practice, not through waiting for a title.

Leadership can also look different from one situation to another. Some people lead by giving clear direction and making fast decisions. Others persuade people around an idea. Some motivate a team, while others become role models through consistent actions and the way they treat people. None of those methods automatically makes one person more of a leader than another. The source curriculum wants you to recognize that different situations need different strengths. A useful leader learns how they tend to lead, understands how other people lead, and chooses the approach that helps the group reach the goal without ignoring the people involved.

The curriculum uses a teamwork video with penguins, ants, and other animals to make one leadership lesson visible. The strength of a group can be greater than the strength of one individual. Pause the video and watch the linked teamwork clip. When you return, focus on one question. What made the smaller individuals successful? The lesson is not that everyone must act the same. It is that people become more effective when they understand their abilities, cooperate, and combine strengths. A community leader should develop personal skills, but also know how to bring different people together so those skills can support one another.

Now you'll use the official leadership type assessment provided with this lesson. The source explains that the assessment contains groups of traits that you rank according to how strongly they describe you, then total the results to see which leadership patterns appear most strongly. Follow the instructions on the ACT worksheet exactly. Do not try to force the result you want. Pause the video and complete the assessment honestly. Treat the result as information about your current tendencies, not as a permanent label. The next lesson will explain the four leadership directions and help you understand what your result actually means in practice.

Before you leave the assessment, remember what a result can and cannot tell you. It can help you notice patterns in how you think, communicate, decide, or work with people. It cannot define your whole identity, and it does not prove that one leadership style is better than another. The source is very clear that different leadership types matter because different situations need different qualities. A result should create self-awareness, not a box. If two patterns seem close, that is not a problem. Human behavior changes across roles and situations, and you will understand that better when we build the leadership compass in the next lesson.

There's one more source activity worth keeping because it gives you a different kind of evidence about yourself. Choose five people whose opinions you trust and ask each one two questions. What are my strengths and what positive traits do you see in me? Your goal is to collect several specific answers, including repeated ones. If the same strength appears more than once, that may be a useful pattern to notice. But remember, these answers are opinions, not final facts about who you are. Pause now to write the names of the five people you will ask, then complete the conversations before the next lesson.

Why ask other people when this unit is about self-awareness? Because leaders need both an internal view and an external one. You know your intentions, but other people experience your behavior. Their feedback can reveal strengths you overlook, or show that a quality you value is visible consistently to others. At the same time, one person's opinion, especially a negative one, does not define you. Use feedback as information to examine, not a verdict to obey. The curriculum connects this research with development. Discover your strengths so you can invest in them. Keep developing undiscovered abilities, and become more useful to the people and projects around you.

Today, you learned that leadership is a skill that can be developed, not one personality you either have or do not have. You learned that people can lead through direction, influence, motivation, example, and teamwork, and that knowing your own tendencies helps you contribute more deliberately. You also completed a leadership assessment, and began collecting outside feedback about your strengths. Keep both results.
