Before we build the leadership compass, look at the feedback you collected from people you trust. Which strengths appeared more than once? Which answer surprised you? The source curriculum reminds us that these are subjective opinions. So, do not treat them as absolute truth. Use them alongside your own assessment result. Self-awareness becomes stronger when you compare what you believe about yourself with the patterns other people consistently experience.

Today, we will organize some of those patterns into a simple leadership model. Not to put you in a box, but to help you understand how different people naturally approach tasks, decisions, and relationships. Picture two lines crossing. The first axis is about interest or focus. On one side, a leader is more focused on completing tasks and getting results. On the other, the leader pays more attention to people, relationships and feelings. The second axis is speed. Some people naturally move and decide quickly, while others prefer a slower more patient approach. When these two axes cross, the curriculum creates four directions. North, South, East, and West. The important idea is not the compass name itself. It is that leadership behavior can be understood through patterns of task focus, people focus, speed, and patience.

North is the fast task focused direction. The curriculum describes Northern leaders as straightforward, results focused, quick to initiate, comfortable with challenges and often independent. Those traits can be useful when a team needs movement, clarity or a decision. But every strength has a risk when overused. Moving quickly can become controlling and strong task focus can cause a leader to overlook how people are feeling. The lesson is not North is good or North is bad. It is that a community leader needs to know when decisiveness helps the situation and when the same instinct needs to be balanced with listening and empathy.

South is more people focused and patient. The source describes southern leaders as good listeners who value relationships, involve others in decisions, like helping people, and consider other people's feelings. That can make a team feel supported and included. But the same pattern may make it harder to say no, move quickly or prioritize a deadline when people are uncomfortable.

East is the detail focused direction, logical, analytical, methodical, careful, and able to concentrate deeply. Those qualities support quality and planning, but too much analysis or perfectionism can slow a decision. Every direction brings both useful strengths and limits. West is the creative and adventurous direction. The curriculum connects it with imagination, motivation, social energy, risk-taking, and generating new ideas. A western style can help a team see possibilities that other people miss. The risk is that creativity can move beyond what is practical or ignore important details.

Put all four directions together and the message becomes clearer. Fast execution, human connection, careful and analysis and creative thinking are all valuable. A strong team may need all four. A community leader should not ask which type is best, but what does this situation need and whose strengths fit that need best? The curriculum also warns us not to turn the compass into a cage. Human beings are complicated and the same person may lead differently at home, at work, with friends, or during a community project. You may have one pattern that appears more often, but you can still use traits from other directions. The source even uses a story where a task focused leader realizes that his behavior hurts someone's feelings, then apologizes and corrects it. That matters. Your leadership style never excuses poor behavior. Self-awareness means recognizing your pattern, noticing its impact, admitting mistakes, and adapting when the situation or the people around you need something different.

Now, apply the compass to a real decision. Think of a community project that suddenly has a problem. The deadline is close, one team member is upset, the plan has missing details and the original idea is no longer working. Pause the video and decide which leadership strength you would call on first. North might push for a decision. South might address the relationship. East might examine the missing details. West might generate a new approach. There is no single correct answer because the situation may need more than one. The skill is recognizing which strength should lead at a particular moment and being willing to let someone else lead when they are the better fit.

This closes unit one and it's question, who am I? You explored your identity, your strengths and weaknesses, the way you work with others and the leadership patterns you naturally use. The source ends this unit with an important decision. Are you ready to lead when your abilities fit the mission and are you also ready to step back when someone else is better suited to lead it? That is mature leadership. Your skills become valuable when you apply them, not when you simply name them.
