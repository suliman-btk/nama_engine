## Scene 01

[natural pace] [interest] [natural]

Hi, and welcome back to Act. So far, most of the attention has been on understanding yourself. Now the view starts to move outward. Every day, you move through different parts of life, and in each one you have a role. You may be a student, a family member, a friend, a neighbor, an employee, or something else. [short pause] These roles are not separate identities. They are different ways you take part in the world around you. Understanding them helps you see not only where you belong, but also how your actions connect you to other people. That connection is the starting point for social awareness.

---

## Scene 02

[natural pace] [thoughtful] [natural]

A role is simply a position you take in a certain part of life. The same person can have many roles in one day. Someone may be a student in the morning, a son or daughter at home, a friend later, and a customer at a shop. [short pause] None of these roles cancels the others. They exist at the same time, and each one changes what is expected in that situation. This is why two people can live in the same place but experience it differently: they may be connected to different people, responsibilities, and communities through the roles they have. A role is not just a label; it shapes how you participate.

---

## Scene 03

[natural pace] [interest] [natural]

Here is the important part: roles usually come with responsibilities. A student is connected to learning, classmates, and the school environment. A family member has responsibilities toward people at home. A neighbor shares a space with other people and affects that space through everyday choices. [short pause] Knowing your roles helps you notice these connections. It does not mean carrying every problem around you. It means understanding that your actions happen inside relationships, and that each role can affect other people in small or large ways. This is where social awareness starts to become practical.

---

## Scene 04

[natural pace] [curiosity] [natural]

Roles also help us understand communities. A community can be thought of as people who share something in common and exist within a shared area or setting. Think about a school: students, teachers, and staff have different roles, but together they form one community. A family is another community. A hospital can be another, with doctors, nurses, staff, and patients connected around a shared place and purpose. [short pause] Your roles are like doors. Each one connects you to a different community, and each community connects you to different people and responsibilities.

---

## Scene 05

[natural pace] [thoughtful] [natural]

A community is more than a list of people. It also includes the environment and things that make life inside it possible. In a school, that may include classrooms, learning materials, facilities, rules, and shared spaces. In a hospital, it can include medicine, equipment, rooms, and systems that support care. [short pause] Looking at a community this way helps you see it as a living system. People, roles, resources, places, and relationships affect one another. When one part changes, other parts of the community may feel the effect. That is why community problems are rarely isolated.

---

## Scene 06

[natural pace] [interest] [natural]

Communities can also sit inside larger communities. A class can be part of a school. A school can be part of an education system. A clinic can be part of a hospital network, and that network can be part of a wider health sector. A neighborhood sits inside a town or city, which sits inside a country. [short pause] This is the extent of a community. The boundary depends on what you are trying to understand. Sometimes the useful view is very small. Other times you need to step back and see the larger system around it. Choosing the right scale makes the picture clearer.

---

## Scene 07

[natural pace] [curiosity] [natural]

Once you know which community you are looking at, the next step is to understand it instead of guessing about it. A simple investigation can ask: what issue exists here? Is it still happening? Has anyone tried to address it before? If so, what did they do? [short pause] You do not need to conduct formal research now. Just notice the habit behind these questions. When you want to understand a community, observation and reliable information are more useful than assumptions. They help you see what is actually happening before deciding what it means. That habit will matter a lot in the next unit.

---

## Scene 08

[slow pace] [hope] [natural]

So the path is becoming clearer: your roles connect you to people, those roles carry responsibilities, and those connections place you inside different communities. Each community has its own people, resources, environment, and boundaries. [short pause] In the next lesson, we will look inside those communities more closely. We will explore what contribution and volunteering mean, how to notice both strengths and problems, and how to build a simple community map that leads directly into the next unit. Keep that map in mind. That’s it for today, and I’ll see you in the next lesson.
