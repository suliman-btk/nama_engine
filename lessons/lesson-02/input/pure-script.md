# prime — Lesson 02: How Project DPro Works Across the Lifecycle

A NAMA Foundation course based on Project DPro, Second Edition (2020)

Approximate duration: 7–8 minutes
Focus: Five connected phases, five principles, decision gates, and MEAL.

## Scene 001 — Welcome Back & The Bigger Picture

Assalamu alaikum warahmatullahi wabarakatuh. Welcome back to Prime. In the first lesson, we looked at why project management matters and why development projects need more than good intentions. Now we need the bigger picture. A project is not simply a list of activities completed one after another. We need to understand the need, prepare the project, plan the work, manage implementation, and eventually close or hand over the project. Project D-Pro gives us a structure for doing this, alongside principles and decision points that guide how we think throughout the journey. Each phase also gives us a question to answer before moving ahead.

## Scene 002 — The Five Connected Phases

Project D-Pro organizes project management into five connected phases. Identification and definition is where we understand the need and define the project. Project setup establishes its authorization and management structure. Project planning turns that direction into an implementation plan. Project implementation involves managing work, people, resources, issues, and changes. Finally, project closure helps the team finish responsibly, including handover, reporting, evaluation, and learning. These phases give us a map for the decisions ahead.

## Scene 003 — Interacting Phases & Adaptive Reality

These phases are not five separate boxes. We do not always finish one, forget it, and move to the next. Project D-Pro explains that they interact. You may think about sustainability and closure while designing the project. During implementation, new information may make you revisit a risk or change the plan. Planning can continue as better information becomes available. The life cycle gives direction, but the work has to respond to the reality of the project. That is why the information from one phase must remain available to the team in another. A plan is useful only while it reflects what the team now knows.

## Scene 004 — The Five Principles

The phases show where we are in a project. Project D-Pro's five principles guide how we manage it in every phase. Well-governed, participatory, comprehensive, integrated, and adaptive. Think of these as habits, not another five steps to complete. You do not finish being participatory after one meeting. You involve the right people when you identify a need, design the work, implement activities, and close the project. The same applies to the other four principles. The principles stay relevant even when the team moves from analysis to delivery. They shape both a small choice and a major project decision.

## Scene 005 — Principle 1: Well-Governed

Well-governed means there is clarity about decisions. Who has authority? Who is accountable? What can the project manager decide? And when must a decision go to a sponsor or board? Without that clarity, even a small change can cause confusion. People may not know who makes the final call, or the manager may seek approval for every detail. Governance sets out responsibilities, authority, tolerances, and decision making so the project can move while remaining accountable. If an issue crosses an agreed tolerance, the manager knows when to bring it to the right decision makers.

## Scene 006 — Principle 2: Participatory

Participatory means giving the right stakeholders meaningful opportunities to contribute. Development projects affect people. They should not be designed entirely from an office. Participation can improve the information we collect, strengthen design, manage expectations, and build ownership. But it does not mean every person attends every meeting or approves every decision. The team needs to consider whose perspective matters for a particular choice and involve them appropriately. We will explore that more closely when we study stakeholders.

## Scene 007 — Principles 3 & 4: Comprehensive and Integrated

Two principles are easy to confuse. Comprehensive means considering the whole project. Activities, stakeholders, risks, resources, communication, sustainability, monitoring, and more. Integrated means these parts work together. A schedule affects resources. Risks may affect the schedule. Stakeholder decisions can affect scope. Monitoring may show that a plan needs to change. These are not separate documents prepared only to fill a folder. They are connected parts of one project and one set of decisions. In the Delta River case, a choice about sanitation would affect community engagement, costs, and the way success is measured.

## Scene 008 — Principle 5: Adaptive

The fifth principle is adaptive. A carefully planned project will still face changes. A risk may become an issue, a stakeholder need may shift, or an assumption may turn out to be wrong. Being adaptive does not mean changing direction whenever someone has a new idea. It means using information to decide whether change is needed, then managing it in a controlled way. Plans and other project documents can be updated as the team learns and the context changes. A team may keep its goal while changing a method or schedule when evidence shows the original approach is no longer suitable.

## Scene 009 — Decision Gates

Project D Pro also uses decision gates, points where the right people review current information and ask, are we still doing the right project in the right way? The decision might be to continue, modify the project, or stop it. Stopping is not automatically failure. If a need has changed or the intervention is no longer appropriate, continuing only because work has already started could make things worse. A gate gives the project a deliberate moment to check its direction. The review needs current evidence and people with the authority to make or approve the next decision.

## Scene 010 — Governance in Practice: The Delta River Latrine Project

Here is governance in the Delta River Latrine project. Rain is identified as a risk that could delay digging the holes. Imagine that it rains for five straight days. The project manager can make decisions about delays of up to three days. Five days is beyond that tolerance, so the manager escalates the issue to the project board. The board reviews the situation and the proposed response then decides how to proceed. The example shows why authority limits matter when a real issue appears. It also gives the manager a clear path when the original response is beyond their authority.

## Scene 011 — Cross-Cutting Discipline: MEAL

One more idea runs through the whole journey. Meal, meaning monitoring, evaluation, accountability, and learning. It is not something the team adds at the end to write a report. Early on, evidence helps us understand the need and decide what to measure. During implementation, monitoring shows progress and problems. Feedback helps us remain accountable to the people affected. Evaluation and learning help us judge results and improve decisions. Think of Meal as information flowing through the project, helping the team act and adapt. We will return to Meal as each project decision takes shape.

## Scene 012 — The Roadmap Ahead

Now you have the map. Five connected phases to guide where the project is going. Five principles to guide how it is managed. Decision gates to check whether to continue. And Meal to support decisions with evidence and learning. Keep the Delta River in mind. Before a team commits to a solution, what does it need to know about the people, the river, and the available services?
