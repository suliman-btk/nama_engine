Hi, and welcome back to Act. Imagine you finished a project plan and every box is filled in. Does that mean the plan is ready? Not always. A plan can look complete and still depend on assumptions that are too optimistic, too expensive, or simply wrong. This is why planning also includes review. Before action starts, it is useful to look at the plan again and ask: what could make this difficult in real life?

Start by checking the parts that depend on assumptions. Maybe you expect one hundred people to attend. Maybe you assume a room is available. Maybe you believe a task will take one day. Maybe you expect a certain resource to be free. None of those assumptions are automatically bad. The problem is treating them like facts before checking them. A stronger plan separates what you know from what you are only expecting.

For example, imagine the plan expects one hundred participants, but the available room only holds forty. The goal may still be useful, but the plan has a mismatch. You could reduce the number, use more than one session, or look for a different location. The important skill is not protecting the first version of the plan. It is noticing the mismatch and adjusting the plan while the change is still easy.

The same applies to resources and cost. Suppose your activity depends on equipment that costs twice the amount you can realistically spend. You now have a decision to make. Can you borrow it? Replace it? Reduce the scope? Change the activity? A weak plan ignores the problem and hopes it disappears. A stronger plan looks for an option that still serves the original need without pretending the limitation is not there.

Time needs the same kind of review. Ask whether tasks are in a logical order and whether one task depends on another. You cannot print materials before the content is ready. You cannot confirm participants before the time and place are clear. When a task depends on something else, that dependency belongs in the plan. A realistic timeline is not only a list of dates. It reflects the order in which the work actually has to happen.

Practice can also expose weaknesses that planning alone may miss. When people repeat a task, they often become faster, notice patterns, and understand what they need more clearly. The useful lesson is simple: before an important action, a small test or rehearsal can reveal missing steps, unclear instructions, or skills that still need work.

Try a short individual check. Imagine a community activity with these details: fifty participants, one small room, no confirmed permission, a very limited budget, and all preparation planned for the final day. What would you question first? Which part needs evidence, a different option, or more time? You do not need a perfect answer. The point is to practice looking at a plan as something that can be tested, not something that must be defended.

A useful plan is not the one that never changes. It is the one that becomes stronger when you check it against reality. Review assumptions, check resources and cost, look for timing problems, test what you can, and improve the weak parts. Next, we will look at another source of better planning: people and environments that already have experience with similar work. They can show you things that are difficult to see from a document alone. I’ll see you in the next lesson.
