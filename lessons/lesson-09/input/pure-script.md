# ACT Lesson 9 -- Pure Locked Narration (derived: bracket cues stripped, no wording changed)

## Scene 01

Hi, and welcome back to Act. You already have a clear problem in front of you. Now comes a very important question: what do the people affected by this problem actually need? It sounds simple, but this is where many projects go wrong. People often choose a solution because it looks useful, modern, or easy to organize. But a good solution should come after the need, not before it. So for now, forget about apps, campaigns, workshops, donations, or any other idea. Focus on the people. What are they struggling with? What is stopping them? And what kind of help would really make a difference?

## Scene 02

Let’s make this easier with a simple example. Think about four very different cases: a tree, a student, a horse, and a carpenter. What does each one need to grow, learn, move, or do the job well? The answers will be different because their situations are different. A hammer may help a carpenter, but it is useless to a horse. A notebook may help a student, but it will not help a tree grow. This sounds obvious, right? But the same mistake happens in community projects. We sometimes give people the tool we like instead of the support they really need. The first job is to understand the person and the situation.

## Scene 03

So what do we mean by a need? Keep it simple: a need is something important that helps a person or group improve their situation. It could be information, a skill, transport, equipment, access to a service, time, support, or something else. The important part is that the need should come from the real problem, not from our guess. For example, if someone cannot use a service, the real need may not be “a new app.” Maybe the problem is that they do not know the service exists, cannot reach it, or do not understand how to use it. Different causes can create very different needs, even when the problem looks similar from the outside.

## Scene 04

Go back to the issue you chose. Ask yourself four questions: who is affected most? What are they unable to do, get, or reach? What seems to be stopping them? And what kind of support could help? Do not rush the answers. Use the information you collected before, and pay attention to what people actually say or experience. Sometimes the people living with the problem will point out something you completely missed. That is useful. You are not trying to prove that your first idea was correct. You are trying to understand the situation better. The clearer the need becomes, the easier it will be to choose a solution that actually fits.

## Scene 05

Here is a quick test. Imagine the people you want to help need training, but you only give them equipment. The equipment may sit there unused. Or maybe they already have the knowledge, but the real problem is transport. Giving them more information will not fix that. A project can look good and still miss the real need. That is why “helping” is not only about doing something. It is about doing something that fits. Sometimes the best support is very simple. Sometimes it is not. The question is always the same: does this really help with the barrier people are facing, or are we just giving them what is easiest for us to provide?

## Scene 06

Now let’s try it with your own issue. Finish this sentence: “The people affected by this issue need…” Write one answer, then look at it again. Is it a need, or did you accidentally write a solution? For example, “They need an app” is already a solution. Ask yourself what the app is supposed to help them do. Maybe the real need is easier access to information, faster communication, or a simpler way to complete a task. When you find the need behind the solution, you open the door to more than one possible answer. That gives you more freedom later, because you can compare different ways to help instead of getting stuck on one idea too early.

## Scene 07

Now compare your problem sentence with your need sentence. They should connect, but they should not say the same thing. The problem tells you what is going wrong. The need tells you what could help the people affected move forward. If the problem is clear but the need still feels weak, that is okay. Go back and learn a little more. If the need does not clearly connect to the problem, check it again. There is no prize for moving fast. Taking extra time here can save you from building the wrong thing later. Good community work is not about having the quickest answer. It is about choosing an answer that makes sense for the people you want to support.

## Scene 08

Before you finish, keep three things together: your problem statement, the useful information you found, and your current need statement. You may still improve them later, and that is normal. For now, the main idea is simple: different people and situations need different kinds of support, and the right tool only works when it matches the real need. Next, we can finally start thinking about solutions. But instead of choosing the first idea that comes to mind, we are going to create several options first. That gives you a much better chance of finding something practical, useful, and worth developing. That’s it for today. Keep your notes with you, and I’ll see you in the next lesson.
